### 11.3 The Economics: Costs, Models, Budgets

You've been collecting `/cost` numbers since Chapter 6 — time to turn them into an operating model. What agentic work actually costs, how model choice moves the bill, when subscriptions beat API pricing, and the only framing that matters commercially: cost *against* what the work returns. Builders who know their numbers price with confidence; builders who don't, guess in both directions and lose both ways.

!!! abstract "What you will learn"
    - Read your real cost structure: tokens, sessions, and what drives each.
    - Match model tiers to task types — the biggest single lever on the bill.
    - Choose between subscription and API pricing for your actual usage.
    - Frame costs the professional way: unit economics per shipped thing.

!!! success "Builder principle"
    Track cost per *outcome*, not per month. "$18 of compute shipped a $900 site" is an operating model. "$60 subscription, seems fine?" is a shrug.

## What you're actually paying for

Everything bills in tokens (2.2) — in and out, across every loop turn. Which makes your cost drivers exactly your *context* drivers, and hands you a delightful fact: **the entire Chapter 5 discipline doubles as cost engineering.**

- Bloated always-on context (a rambling CLAUDE.md) is a tax on *every* session — trim it and the savings recur (5.2's "every token earns its slot," now denominated in money).
- Marathon unscoped sessions re-send their swollen history every turn — 5.5's session shapes are directly cheaper.
- Exploration debris in the main window is paid-for noise — subagents (10.1) quarantine the spend *and* return only conclusions.
- Failed approaches retried in a polluted window burn tokens on confusion — the 6.3 clean-restart move is a cost control too.

You optimized all of this for *quality* chapters ago. The invoice was agreeing with you the whole time.

## The model tier lever

From 2.5: model families come in tiers — fast-and-cheap (Haiku class), the workhorse (Sonnet class), maximum-capability (Opus class and up) — with *order-of-magnitude* price differences between the ends. The matching skill:

| Task type | Tier | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine, well-harnessed: formatting, summaries, log triage, homogeneous fan-out chunks (10.1) | Fast | The harness catches misses; capability is surplus |
| Daily building — most of your work | Workhorse | The quality/cost sweet spot; it's the default for a reason |
| Architecture, hard debugging, split-planning (10.3), anything where a wrong call is expensive | Max | One right answer beats three cheap retries — retries cost tokens *and* your attention |

Claude Code lets you set and switch models (`/model`, and per-subagent in role definitions — your 10.1 crew can run cheap researchers under a workhorse orchestrator; that composition is genuinely how pros run fleets). The anti-pattern in both directions has a name from 4.4: it's the verification dial, mispriced — max-tier for typo fixes is vanity; budget-tier for architecture is false economy that bills you in rework.

## Subscription vs. API

Two ways to pay, and the right one is usage-math, not ideology:

- **Subscription** (Claude Pro/Max tiers): flat monthly, generous included usage, predictable. Wins for: steady daily building — most course graduates, most freelancers. You've probably been here all course; `/cost` has been showing you what you'd have paid raw.
- **API pricing**: pure pay-per-token. Wins for: spiky usage, headless/scheduled automation (11.2's cron-run health checks bill per run, not per seat), and anything you're running *for clients* where cost should be attributable per project.
- **The graduation pattern:** subscription for your hands-on work, plus a small API budget for automation — most working setups land here eventually. Revisit when either line item dominates.

## Unit economics: the framing that matters

The monthly bill is the wrong unit of analysis. The right one:

```
Per shipped thing:
  compute cost        (session costs, from your /cost receipts)
+ your hours × your rate
= cost of goods
vs. what it returned  (fee, hours saved × rate, career value)
```

Run it on your 6.2 recipe: a client site at ~$15–40 of compute and, at your *current* speed, maybe 6–10 hours of your time. Against even modest local-market pricing ($500–1,500), the margin is real — and every Skill you ratcheted (7.4's "library is the margin") and every hour your harness saves widens it. This is the arithmetic behind the whole 11.4 conversation, so keep receipts: from now on, journal `compute + hours` per shipped project. Three projects of data makes you the rare freelancer who *knows* their cost of goods.
