### 4.2 Plan Mode: Think Before You Build

The cheapest place to catch a mistake is before it exists. Plan mode is Claude Code's built-in way to enforce that: the agent can read and think, but can't touch anything until you approve its approach. This lesson teaches you when to demand a plan, how to review one like a pro, and why the plan-first habit multiplies quality on anything bigger than a tweak.

!!! abstract "What you will learn"
    - Use plan mode: what it locks, what it allows, how to enter and exit it.
    - Decide task-by-task whether planning is worth it (the 10-minute rule).
    - Review a plan with the four questions that catch bad approaches early.

!!! success "Builder principle"
    Approve approaches, not just outcomes. A plan review costs two minutes; unwinding a wrong approach costs the afternoon.

## What plan mode is

In plan mode, the agent's *act* phase is disabled: it can gather freely — read files, search, analyze — but instead of building, it produces a proposal: here's what I'd do, in what order, touching which files, with what trade-offs. Nothing changes on disk until you approve.

In Claude Code you toggle it with ++shift+tab++ (or start a session that way). The agent researches, presents a plan, and waits. Approve it, and it executes — now with a spine.

Why this works so well mechanically: the plan itself enters the context window, so during execution the agent is following *its own written commitments* instead of improvising each step. Drift drops dramatically. You've effectively pinned the whiteboard (Lesson 2.2) with an agenda.

## When to plan: the 10-minute rule

Planning everything is bureaucracy; planning nothing is chaos. The working rule:

> **If the task would take a competent human more than ~10 focused minutes, or touches more than a couple of files, or is hard to undo — plan first.**

| Skip the plan | Demand the plan |
| --- | --- |
| Fix a typo, adjust a color | Add a new page or feature |
| Rename one thing | Restructure folders |
| One-file, obvious change | Anything touching data, config, or dependencies |
| You could do it yourself in 2 min | You couldn't do it yourself at all (!) |

That last row matters most for the no-code lane: **the less you could verify the execution, the more you must verify the approach.** The plan is written in English — you can *always* judge English.

## Reviewing a plan: the four questions

A plan lands. Don't skim-approve it — interrogate it:

**1. Does it match my intent?** Read the plan's goal statement back against your brief. Mismatch here means your brief leaked ambiguity — cheaper to fix now than after the build.

**2. Is the blast radius right?** Which files does it plan to touch? A "add a contact form" plan that modifies twelve files is answering a question you didn't ask. Challenge it: *"Why does step 3 touch the database config?"*

**3. What's the riskiest step?** Every plan has one — a dependency install, a data migration, a big refactor step. Find it, and ask what happens if it goes wrong. Good agents answer with rollback paths; that conversation alone often improves the plan.

**4. What's missing?** Usually it's verification. If the plan ends with "…and it's done" instead of "…and we confirm by X," add the X: *"Include a final step: run it and show me evidence."*

Then approve — explicitly, having actually read it. Two minutes, every time, forever.

## Iterating on plans

The underrated move: plans are drafts, and negotiating them is free.

```text
Good plan overall. Changes: skip step 4 (I don't want a newsletter
signup yet), and in step 2 use the same card style as the Services
section. Also: what would you cut if I only wanted the simplest
version that works?
```

That last question — *what would you cut?* — is one of the highest-value prompts in agentic work. Agents, like eager contractors, over-scope by default. Asking for the minimal version first, shipping it, then expanding is the professional rhythm (and it's your scope-killer from Lesson 1.4 as a repeatable move).

## Try it now

Time to open your **quick win** for real. In its project folder:

1. Enter plan mode (++shift+tab++).
2. Give the full five-element brief you wrote in Lesson 2.3.
3. When the plan arrives, run all four questions *in writing* in your journal — one line each.
4. Negotiate at least one change, and add a verification step if it lacked one.
5. Approve, and let it build. Post-flight as always (Lesson 3.5).

You just ran the professional-grade version of the loop: brief → plan → negotiate → execute → verify. That sequence is the backbone of every serious build in the rest of this course.
