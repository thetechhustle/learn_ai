# 12.2 Read the Blueprint and Build a Study Plan

An exam outline is not a reading list. It is a compressed description of decisions
you may need to make under constraints. The fastest way to waste study time is to
collect pages and vocabulary without translating them into performance.

!!! abstract "What you will learn"
    - Convert exam domains into observable capabilities.
    - Build a study matrix that joins video, documentation, lab, and evidence.
    - Decompose long scenario questions without guessing what the author intended.
    - Choose a sustainable study cadence for a beginner, practitioner, or engineer.

!!! success "Builder principle"
    Every study block needs a verb, a lab, and a check. “Read about agents” is not a plan.

its nouns. You need to show an action, a constraint, and a verification method.

## Decompose the scenario

Long certification questions often contain several layers. Before looking at the
answer choices, write a one-line answer to each:

1. **Actor:** Who is using the system, and who could be affected?
2. **Outcome:** What must happen, and what must not happen?
3. **Context:** What information is available, missing, stale, or untrusted?
4. **Tools:** Which action requires a tool, and which can stay informational?
5. **Permission:** What can be read, written, sent, purchased, or changed?
6. **Constraint:** Is the priority correctness, latency, cost, privacy, reliability, or portability?
7. **Failure:** What happens if the model is wrong, the tool is unavailable, or the output is malformed?
8. **Verification:** What independent evidence would justify acceptance?

Then compare choices against the stated constraints. The “most sophisticated” design
is often wrong when a simpler design meets the outcome with less permission and less
operational surface.

## A sustainable cadence

The video suggests that beginners may need substantially more time than experienced
developers and recommends balancing lectures, labs, and practice questions. Use that
as a planning prompt, not a deadline. Choose a cadence you can sustain:

### Six-week route

- **Week 1:** readiness brief, access check, vocabulary, and domain matrix.
- **Week 2:** SDK or simulation setup; agent loop and tool-use lab.
- **Week 3:** orchestration, context, and state; compare sequential and parallel designs.
- **Week 4:** MCP, permissions, prompt/output contracts, and failure injection.
- **Week 5:** reliability lab, scenario practice, and peer review.
- **Week 6:** timed-but-not-rushed mixed practice, source recheck, and exam decision.

### Three-week intensive route

- **Days 1–3:** map domains and identify the two weakest capabilities.
- **Days 4–10:** complete one implementation or simulation per domain.
- **Days 11–15:** run the evidence pack, scenario defenses, and reviewer pass.
- **Days 16–21:** mixed practice, targeted repair, and access/format recheck.

Do not schedule a high-stakes exam just because the calendar ended. Schedule when
your evidence shows that the remaining gap is ordinary uncertainty, not an untested
core capability or an unresolved access question.

### No-code lane

Use architecture cards. Each card has: goal, context, tools, permissions, failure,
verification, and escalation. Draw the flow on paper or in a document. A good card
can be reviewed without access to your account.

### Engineer lane

Create a disposable repository with a `study/` directory. Store fixtures, schemas,
test cases, and results. Keep provider credentials outside the repository and do not
use production data. Your matrix should link each performance claim to an executable
or simulated check.

## Using AI to study without outsourcing judgment

An assistant can help you generate variations, explain vocabulary, or act as a
skeptical reviewer. It should not become your answer key. Use a two-pass pattern:

1. Answer the scenario yourself and save it.
2. Ask the assistant to identify missing constraints, unsafe assumptions, and a stronger alternative.
3. Verify every provider-specific claim in current primary documentation.
4. Revise your answer and record what changed.

Prompt pattern:

~~~text
Act as a skeptical reviewer of this architecture answer.
Do not give me the correct answer first.
Identify:
1. an unstated constraint,
2. a permission or data-exposure risk,
3. a failure mode I did not test,
4. one simpler alternative,
5. the evidence that would change your recommendation.
Separate durable architecture principles from product-specific facts that need
current documentation.
~~~

## Facilitator talking points

- “What does this domain ask you to do, not merely define?”
- “What is the smallest artifact that would make your answer inspectable?”
- “Which distractor would become correct if the priority changed from cost to safety?”
- “What information is absent from the scenario, and what assumption are you making?”
- “What would you test before you trust your own answer?”
